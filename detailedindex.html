A

Words that create PICTURES & FEELINGS

These tools help the poem make clear pictures and feelings in your mind. Think: sight, smell, touch, and hidden meanings.

  • Imagery — Easy: words that paint pictures in your head using the five senses. Example: "The salty wind stung my face." → you can feel & smell the sea.
  • Symbol (Symbolism) — Easy: one thing stands for something more. It’s a simple object with a deeper message. Example: "A white dove" → stands for peace.
  • Theme — Easy: the poem’s main idea — the message the poet wants you to keep. Poem about kindness → theme = kindness.
  • Tone — Easy: the poet’s feeling while writing (like the teacher’s voice). If lines use happy words → tone = cheerful.
  • Mood — Easy: how the poem makes you feel when you read it. A gentle poem may make the reader feel calm → mood = calm.
Trick: Theme = WHAT it says; Tone = poet’s feeling; Mood = how you feel.
B

The SOUND — music of the poem

Poems often act like music. These parts give them beat, rhyme, and nice sounds.

  • Rhyme — Easy: when line endings sound the same. Makes the poem sing. "Night / Light / Bright" → rhyme is clear.
  • Rhythm — Easy: the beat or flow when you read the poem aloud. "Jack and Jill went up the hill" has a steady rhythm.
  • Alliteration — Easy: same first sound in several words. It makes lines catchy. "She sells sea shells" → 's' sound repeats.
  • Onomatopoeia — Easy: words that copy real sounds. "Buzz", "Crash", "Splash".
  • Meter — Easy: a repeating pattern of strong and weak beats in a line. Many old poems have steady meter so they sound like music.
Trick: Rhyme + Rhythm = poem’s music. Alliteration & onomatopoeia are small sound tools.
C

Language MAGIC — Figures of Speech

These are quick tricks poets use to make lines strong and memorable. Short, bright, and powerful.

  • Simile — Easy: a comparison using "like" or "as". "Brave as a lion." → simile says 'as'.
  • Metaphor — Easy: direct comparison without "like/as". "He is a lion." → means brave, not really a lion.
  • Personification — Easy: giving human actions to things that aren’t human. "The moon smiled." → moon cannot smile, but it is described like a person.
  • Irony — Easy: when something happens that is the opposite of what we expect. "A fire station burns down." → surprising opposite event.
  • Oxymoron — Easy: two opposite words together to make a striking phrase. "Deafening silence" or "bittersweet".
Trick: Simile & Metaphor = comparison team; Personification gives life to objects; Irony surprises us.
D

Kinds (Genres) of Poetry — the poem’s style or purpose

These explain WHAT poems do — tell a story, praise someone, be sad, or simply sing feelings.

  • Lyric — Easy: short poem of personal feeling, like a song lyric. A short love poem that says "I miss you" in lovely words.
  • Narrative — Easy: poem that tells a story with events and characters. A poem describing a journey or adventure.
  • Epic — Easy: very long poem about heroes and big events. Ancient poems about wars and heroes (long and grand).
  • Elegy — Easy: a sad poem written for someone who died. A poem written after a friend passes away.
  • Ode — Easy: a respectful poem that praises someone or something. A poem praising a tree, a hero, or a song.
  • Pastoral — Easy: poems about countryside, farms, and simple life in nature. Poems about fields, shepherds, and sunsets.
  • Ballad — Easy: story-poem often meant to be sung, with repeated lines. Folk songs that tell a story in simple words.
  • Free Verse — Easy: poems with no fixed rhyme or meter — speech-like and free. Modern poems that read like spoken words.
  • Limerick — Easy: a very short funny poem of five lines with a special beat (AABBA rhyme). Classic silly five-line poems.
Memory phrase: Lyric Narrates Epic Elegies, Odes, Pastoral Ballads, Free Verse, Limericks.

Short Story: How these parts work together

Imagine a short poem about a child on a beach:

  • Imagery: "salt on lips, sand warm under feet" — you can smell and feel it.
  • Tone: gentle and happy — the poet uses soft words.
  • Theme: childhood joy — the main message.
  • Form: lyric — short and musical.
  • Figure: "the sea sang" — personification (sea can't sing but it's a nice image).
When you write or read, ask: What does it say? How does it sound? How does it make me feel?

Mini Cheatsheet

One-line reminders you can memorize fast:

  • Imagery = senses → picture in the mind.
  • Symbol = small object with big meaning.
  • Theme = the lesson.
  • Tone = poet’s voice.
  • Mood = reader’s feeling.
  • Rhyme/Rhythm = music.
  • Simile/Metaphor = comparisons (simile uses like/as).
  • Personification = life to things.
  • Genres = purpose (lyric, elegy, ode, etc.).
